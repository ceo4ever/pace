## Looks at

Whether the document leads with its answer and supports it with grouped, non-overlapping points, and whether the opening frames Situation → Complication → Question → Answer.

## Key questions

- What is the one message the reader must take away?
- What situation does the reader already accept, what changed (complication), and what question does that raise?
- Do the supporting points answer that question, sit at the same level, and not overlap (MECE)?
- Is each point backed by facts, not adjectives?

## Common gaps

- The conclusion sits at the end or is missing (a chronological activity list).
- Supporting points mix levels (a goal next to a task) or overlap.
- The complication is unstated, so the reader does not see why this matters now.

## Turning gaps into questions

The key message usually exists in the request or the facts; propose it and ask only if two messages are equally plausible.
Example: "이번 주 보고의 한 줄 메시지는? ① 결제 연동이 예정대로 50% — 다음 주 완료 예상(추천 — 주신 내용이 진행 중심) ② 로그인 버그 해결로 장애 위험 해소 ③ 신입 온보딩으로 인력 늘어남 — 맨 위 문장이 바뀌어요."
When the complication is unknown, assume "없음(정기 보고)" and label it.

## Tensions

- With **audience**: a formal reader may want background first — keep SCQA in the opening paragraph even when the body follows the house order.
- With **document skeletons**: a template's section order may bury the answer — put the answer in the summary line at the top.

## When not to use

Pure reference material (glossaries, specs) and documents whose structure is fixed by law or a form.

## Output form

Form: SCQA opening (four short lines) + pyramid outline (answer → 2–4 grouped points → evidence).
Link to first layer: the key message is the Answer; the complication question decides which facts go up the pyramid.
When to produce: every report or proposal; for L0 only the answer line and grouped bullets.

## Closing

End with the top sentence written out, and every point checked against it (each point answers the question, no overlap).
